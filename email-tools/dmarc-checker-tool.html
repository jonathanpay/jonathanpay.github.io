Common failures

Why legitimate email fails authentication

Most authentication problems trace back to a handful of DNS mistakes. These are the five we see most often — each with the exact fix.

No SPF record at all

Without SPF, receiving servers have no way to know which services are allowed to send email as your domain — so spoofed mail looks no different from yours. Publish one SPF TXT record at the root of your sending domain, listing every service that sends on your behalf.

Copy-paste fix — TXT record

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:servers.mcsv.net ~all

Swap the include: entries for your actual providers — these examples cover Google Workspace and Mailchimp.

Two or more SPF records

The SPF specification allows exactly one record per domain. Publish two and receiving servers return a permanent error — which is worse than having none, because it fails silently while looking configured. Merge every provider into a single record.

Copy-paste fix — one merged record

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net ~all

DKIM never switched on

Most email platforms won't sign with your domain until you add the DNS records they generate and click verify. Until then, your mail is signed with their shared domain — or not at all — and it can't pass DMARC alignment.

There's no generic record to paste here, because every provider generates its own keys: open your platform's domain authentication settings, add the CNAME or TXT records it gives you, and verify. Most providers make this a one-click step once the records are in place.

No DMARC record

Without a DMARC policy, mail that fails SPF and DKIM can still be delivered as if it were yours, you receive no reports about who is sending as your domain — and bulk senders fail the authentication requirements Gmail and Yahoo have enforced since 2024. Start with a monitoring policy and a reporting address.

Copy-paste fix — TXT record at _dmarc

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com

Parked at p=none forever

Monitoring mode is a starting point, not a destination — it tells receiving servers to do nothing when authentication fails, so it offers no protection against spoofing. Once a few weeks of reports show your legitimate mail passing, tighten the policy.

Copy-paste fix — tightened policy

v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com; pct=100

Move to p=reject when you're confident nothing legitimate is failing.

Good to know

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, explained properly

What is SPF?

Sender Policy Framework: a DNS TXT record listing which mail servers are authorised to send email on behalf of your domain. Receiving servers check the sending server against that list before your message gets anywhere near the inbox.

What is DKIM?

DomainKeys Identified Mail: a cryptographic signature added to every outgoing message. Your platform signs with a private key, and receiving servers verify it against the public key published in your DNS — proving the message really came from your domain and wasn't altered in transit.

What is DMARC?

Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance: the policy that ties SPF and DKIM together. It tells receiving servers what to do with mail that fails both checks — nothing (p=none), send it to spam (p=quarantine), or refuse it (p=reject) — and where to send reports about who is sending as your domain.

Why do I need all three?

They solve different problems: SPF authorises servers, DKIM proves message integrity, and DMARC adds enforcement and visibility. Without DMARC, failing the first two carries no consequence for a spoofer.

It's also no longer optional at scale: since 2024, Gmail and Yahoo have required bulk senders to authenticate with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, and Microsoft introduced matching requirements for Outlook in 2025.

What's a DKIM selector, and why can't you always find mine?

The selector is the name your DKIM key is published under — selector._domainkey.yourdomain.com. There's no public registry of selectors, so any checker can only try common ones. If none of the ten we try resolve, that's inconclusive rather than a failure: check your email platform's authentication settings for the selector it uses, and enter it in the field above.

What is DMARC alignment?

DMARC doesn't just ask whether SPF or DKIM passed — it asks whether the domain that passed matches the From address your reader actually sees. An SPF pass on your platform's bounce domain does nothing for DMARC if your From address is your own domain. This is why "SPF: pass" in a header doesn't guarantee a DMARC pass, and why DKIM signing with your own domain matters so much.

Does passing all three guarantee inbox placement?

No. Authentication is the entry ticket, not the ranking — placement still depends on your sending reputation, list quality, engagement, and content. But the reverse is increasingly true: failing authentication does guarantee trouble, from spam-foldering to outright rejection.

Which DMARC policy should I use?

Follow the progression: start at p=none with a rua reporting address and watch the reports; move to p=quarantine once legitimate mail is passing cleanly; finish at p=reject when you're confident. Each step up closes the door further on anyone spoofing your domain — just don't skip the monitoring stage, or you risk quarantining your own mail.
