## Executive Summary

Jon Pay's personal voice is the root from which everything else grows. It's the voice of someone who has worked in email marketing his entire adult life, survived a stroke, thinks seriously about faith, plays RPGs, and has no interest in performing expertise. The writing sounds like thinking happening out loud — grounded, direct, occasionally wry, and specific to the point where it couldn't belong to anyone else.

The most important structural distinction is between articles and posts. Articles are composed; posts are thinking in public. The rules are genuinely different, and treating them as interchangeable flattens both.

## We Are / We Are Not

| We Are | We Are Not |
|--------|------------|
| **Grounded** — every claim anchored in lived experience or specific detail | **Abstract** — no floating generalisations or unanchored emotional language |
| **Direct** — we say the thing without hedging for approval | **Blunt without warmth** — directness is honest, not cold |
| **Conversational** — reads like speaking, not writing for performance | **Casual to the point of sloppy** — conversational means natural, not imprecise |
| **Witty when it fits** — humour arrives inside a thought, not announced | **Flippant** — light, not lightweight |
| **Reflective** — comfortable with complexity; willing to acknowledge nuance | **Sentimental** — conviction is welcome; performed depth is not |
| **Authoritative through specificity** — expertise is shown, not claimed | **Self-promotional** — the detail does the work |

### Voice Attributes Detail

#### Grounded
- **What it means**: Every claim is tethered to a specific memory, a named example, a number, or a concrete situation. No floating generalisations.
- **How it shows up**: Real biographical detail. Specific years, specific scenarios, specific feelings described through precise observation rather than emotional labels.
- **What to avoid**: "Many people find..." / "Society today expects..." / "We live in a world where..."
- **Evidence**: "add a full-time job, a kid, and a stroke, and suddenly I realised… maybe I just liked having energy." — specific detail earns emotional weight.
- **Confidence**: High

#### Direct
- **What it means**: Commit to the point. The shortest path. No hedging unless genuine uncertainty exists. If something is wrong, say so.
- **How it shows up**: Declarative sentences. Active voice. Short punchy conclusions after longer reasoning.
- **What to avoid**: "It could be argued that..." / "Perhaps..." / "One might consider..." / any modifier that can be cut without changing meaning.
- **Evidence**: Style guide: "Hedge only when genuine uncertainty exists."
- **Confidence**: High

#### Witty When It Fits
- **What it means**: Humour is natural, not performed. It arrives mid-sentence, in an aside, in a self-correction — not in a "here's the funny part" setup.
- **How it shows up**: "Well, except for the rogue, who ignored every plan." / "Without whom I wouldn't be in email marketing, let alone exist." The joke is tucked inside the thought.
- **What to avoid**: Forced lightness. Announced humour. Anything that sets up the joke before delivering it.
- **Evidence**: Style guide: "Wit embedded mid-sentence, not announced."
- **Confidence**: High

#### Reflective
- **What it means**: Willing to sit with complexity. Won't wrap everything in a tidy moral conclusion. Self-correction is a technique, not an error (in posts specifically).
- **How it shows up**: Posts catching themselves mid-thought and refining. Endings that land as observations rather than summaries. Articles that resolve without oversimplifying.
- **What to avoid**: "Life is a journey..." / Closing slogans that flatten what preceded them.
- **Evidence**: Style guide: "Conviction is welcome. Artificial neatness is not."
- **Confidence**: High

## The Articles vs Posts Distinction

This is the most important structural rule for Jon's writing.

**Articles** are composed and authoritative. Arguments resolve. Reasoning is sequenced. The voice is still Jon's — grounded, direct, human — but the writing has been shaped. Productive messiness does not belong here. Articles should not have self-corrections, trailing thoughts, or mid-paragraph pivots. They read like something that was written and then considered.

**Posts** (LinkedIn, Bluesky, Substack Notes) are thinking happening in public. The same ideas, but arriving less cleanly. Posts can catch themselves mid-thought, change direction, and leave a conclusion slightly open. The reader should feel like they're watching Jon work something out, not receiving a polished summary of conclusions already reached.

A post that reads like an article has lost something. An article that reads like a post has lost something different.

## Productive Messiness (Posts Only)

The most characteristic Jon Pay writing reads like thinking assembled in real time. Five things in particular:

1. **Connective tissue that's slightly imperfect.** "By extension then" wouldn't survive a strict structural edit. It would also sound exactly like Jon, so leave it. Cut mechanical crutches ("moreover," "furthermore" when they're structural rather than genuine). Keep considered-but-loose connectives.

2. **Wit embedded mid-sentence.** The joke arrives inside the thought, not after a setup.

3. **Asides that feel occurred-to, not allocated.** If something belongs in parentheses, it should read like it occurred to you while writing.

4. **Self-correction as technique.** "Which is a different thing entirely." / "And honestly that's on the emails, not her." / An "actually" that lands mid-realisation. These are texture, not errors. Preserve in posts; resolve in articles.

5. **Wandering and catching himself.** Posts can digress and then return: "I'm a little off course, back to my point." The digression isn't the error — it's part of the thinking-aloud texture. Don't smooth it away.

6. **Thinks in series, not essays.** He opens questions and commits to returning to them rather than resolving them in the current piece ("over a series of posts I would like to look at..."). This is a genuine voice trait. A post that ends without fully resolving its question isn't incomplete — it may be exactly right. AI tends to close the loop; resist that where the unresolved ending is the more honest one.

7. **Questions left genuinely open.** In unassisted writing, rhetorical questions are real invitations into a subject, not setups for an immediate answer. "What would the psychological repercussions be?" gets asked and left there. AI-assisted writing tends to pose a question and answer it in the next sentence. Watch for this: if a question is answered immediately, consider whether leaving it open serves the piece better.

## Format Rules

### Very Short (under 300 words)
A legitimate format, not a draft waiting to be expanded. Some posts in the archive are 80 words and complete. When the thing has been said, it's been said. There is no minimum length requirement. Resist the urge to pad to a "proper" length.

### Short-Form (300–500 words)
- No headings.
- No bullet points.
- Natural storytelling flow.
- Occasional one-line paragraph for pacing — not as a habit.
- Fragments allowed for rhythm, not drama.

### Long-Form (800+ words)
- Headings to break sections logically.
- Open with framing sentences before any list or section.
- Maintain narrative warmth within structural clarity.
- Avoid lecture cadence.

### Endings (all formats)
- Gentle invitation or open reflection.
- No forced moral bow.
- End on an observation, not a summary.
- No closing slogans. A last line that resolves too neatly into a tagline flattens what preceded it. Let the final thought arrive as something that occurred to you.
- In personal writing especially: posts can just end when the thought runs out. "Thats kinda all I have for the moment..." is a legitimate closing. The natural endpoint often arrives well before the formal conclusion.

## Platform-Specific Rules

### Blog / Website / Tumblr
- Primary space for long-form, reflective, or educational content.
- Posts read like small essays or journal entries, not announcements.
- Titles: curious or reflective, not salesy.
- End with a gentle invitation or open reflection (not a CTA or summary).

### LinkedIn
- Open with a hook or human observation (not stats, not sales).
- Short paragraphs (2–3 lines max).
- Stated perspective — not crowd-pleasing.
- Engage through genuine curiosity: "Have you seen this pattern too?"
- Emojis: sparingly, for tone balance only.
- Posts should feel like thinking in public, not polished conclusions.
- When resharing: always add context — why it matters or connects.

### Substack / *Living the Scandalous Life*
- Full essays for the publication.
- Substack Notes for early thinking, observations, and drawing readers to the essay.
- Notes: substance over cleverness; front-load the hook; end naturally.

### Bluesky (300 chars) / Mastodon (500 chars)
- One thought, observation, or question per post.
- Wry, considered, not impulsive.
- No hashtags unless genuinely functional.
- For threads: number posts 1/ (open-ended) or 1/4 (fixed).
- Light overlap between posts — a brief callback and a forward signal.
- Do not cross-post LinkedIn content to Bluesky without adaptation. The ambient register is different; LinkedIn-format posts in a Bluesky feed signal the post was written for somewhere else.
- Avoid posting multiple posts on the same professional theme in a single day. A cluster reads as a scheduled content dump rather than thinking in real time. Spread across days.
- On replies: answer once, fully. If the thread has run through detailed tangents past the natural endpoint, stop. Not every reply needs to be complete.

### Faith Writing
- Reflective, not reactive.
- Calm conviction — no culture-war framing.
- Love and inclusion foregrounded.
- No writing primarily in opposition to "the world" or contemporary culture.
- Lived detail over abstraction. Invitation over persuasion.
- Comfortable with inconclusiveness. Faith writing doesn't need to resolve. Leave room for the thought to stay open.
- Self-awareness should be playful rather than earnest. The "hoo boy, I got that wrong" energy — lightly annotative, not sustained self-indictment.
- Pop culture references appear naturally in this register. Don't force them out for solemnity.

### Dating Profiles & Apps

**Purpose:** Present Jon as a real, specific person — not an optimised pitch. The goal is to invite the right people in and not attract the wrong ones.

#### Profile Writing (bio, prompts, about me)
- Specific over generic. "I run RPG campaigns for friends" beats "I love games." Specificity is what makes a profile worth reading.
- Warm but not eager. The tone is open and self-assured — curious about the other person, not performing likability.
- Wit is welcome when it's natural. Same rule as always: the joke arrives inside the thought, not announced.
- Don't list traits; show them. "I'm adventurous" is nothing. "I once spent a weekend redesigning a friend's whole email list" shows something.
- British English, contractions, no buzzwords — same as everything else.
- No self-deprecation as a bid for approval. Honest humility is fine; fishing for reassurance isn't.
- Don't over-explain or caveat. Trust the reader to engage with what's actually there.
- Let complexity exist. Faith, stroke recovery, being a dad, working in email marketing, playing RPGs — these are all true at once and none of them need to be softened.

#### Prompt Responses
- Answer the actual question. Don't use the prompt as a springboard for a tangent that avoids it.
- One honest thing beats three safe things.
- End on something that invites a response — not with a question appended, but with something genuinely interesting or open-ended.

#### Conversation / Messaging
- Match the energy of the conversation, not a script.
- Genuine curiosity over clever openers. Ask something real.
- Same directness as everywhere else — don't over-hedge.

#### Dating App Social Posts (where available)
- Treat like Bluesky: one honest moment, not a highlight reel.
- Reflective and specific — something that happened, something noticed, something real.
- Not aspirational. Not performing a version of life that's better than the actual one.
- Authenticity over polish, always.

**Tone test:** Does this sound like Jon talking to someone he's genuinely interested in? Or does it sound like Jon trying to seem appealing?

### RPG / Hobby Reviews
Follow this six-section structure:
1. Introduction: how the game was discovered and first impressions.
2. Setting & Theme: what makes the world compelling.
3. Gameplay & Mechanics: ease of learning and playability.
4. Personal Highlights: favourite moments and experiences.
5. Who It's For: best fit for different play styles.
6. Final Thoughts: why it stays in the collection.

## Core Grammar and Style Rules

- British English spelling throughout.
- Oxford comma always.
- Contractions are welcome.
- Italics for emphasis only. No bold for tone.
- No dashes for breaks — use commas or parentheses.
- Default to "I" not "we" unless the experience is genuinely shared.
- Do not universalise personal experience.
- Do not assume reader agreement.
- Vary sentence length deliberately: short for emphasis, longer for nuance.
- Fragments are fine when they serve rhythm or emphasis (not drama).
- **Stop one sentence sooner.** The natural endpoint almost always arrives before the actual endpoint. When the thought feels complete, stop.

## Language to Avoid

### Banned Phrases
- "Quiet surrender," "quiet confidence," and all "quiet [emotion]" constructions.
- "Quietly" as an adverb adding atmosphere ("quietly fades," "quietly loses").
- "Journey" unless literally referring to travel or narrative.
- "In today's world..." / "The truth is..."
- "Push back gently" / "worth unpacking" — LLM-signalling.
- Vague societal abstractions: "society tells us," "people are tired of," "many believe." Ground the claim or drop it.

### Banned Buzzwords
- Ever-evolving, fast-paced, game-changing, innovative, revolutionary, transformative, unprecedented, groundbreaking.
- Empower, unlock, synergy, solution-driven.
- Vibrant, nestled, showcasing — promotional/atmospheric adjectives that signal AI-generated copy.

### LLM-Signalling Openers
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "In a world where..."
- "Let's dive in."
- "At the end of the day..."
- "Now more than ever..."
- "Have you ever wondered..." (as an article opener)

### Filler Signposting
- Delve into / dive into / deep dive.
- It's worth noting / it's important to note.
- Bear in mind / keep in mind (as openers).
- It goes without saying.
- Without further ado.
- Certainly, (as a sentence opener).
- Underscore (as a verb meaning "emphasise") — high-frequency AI tell.

### Overused Modifiers
- Crucial / it's crucial that.
- Notable / notably.
- Significant / significantly (as filler emphasis).
- Pivotal — AI-frequency word; signals inflated importance.
- Very, quite, rather, somewhat, fairly, extremely, highly, particularly — run the qualifiers test: remove each one and see if meaning changes. If not, cut it.

### Structural Patterns to Avoid
- **Artificial contrast**: "It's not about X, it's about Y." State the argument directly.
- **Rule of three by default**: Break the pattern. Use two, use five, or write it as a sentence.
- **Constant hedging**: "Testing tells you what works. That's it." — not "Testing might help you understand what could be working better."
- **Passive voice as default**: "The subscriber opened the email" — not "The email was opened by the subscriber." Passive is fine when it genuinely reads better.
- **Unnecessary definitions**: "Segmentation improves engagement. Your audience isn't one person." — not "Email segmentation (the practice of dividing your list into smaller groups) can improve engagement."
- **Performed pauses**: "Let that sink in." / "Let that sit for a second." Trust the point to land.
- **Closing slogans**: A conclusion that resolves too neatly into a tagline. Let the last line arrive as a thought.
- **Uniform paragraph length**: A mechanical smoothness that signals AI cadence. Vary structure.
- **"Serves as" instead of "is"**: AI avoids simple copulas in favour of "serves as," "functions as," "acts as." Prefer direct "is/are" unless the longer form genuinely adds meaning.
- **Negative parallelism**: "Not just X, but also Y." Related to artificial contrast but additive rather than reframing — flag and simplify.
- **Superficial analysis via trailing -ing clauses**: Participial phrases tacked on to a statement to gesture at depth ("making this a pivotal moment for the industry"). These often constitute unattributed opinion dressed as observation. If the claim isn't sourced or grounded, cut the clause.
- **AI-introduced formalism in personal writing**: When writing personal content with AI assistance, watch for formal section headers, sustained evidential argument structure, and earnest self-indictment presented as rhetoric. These are patterns AI tends to introduce that fight against the natural personal voice, which is lighter, more self-deprecating, and more comfortable with wandering. The unassisted register is closer to thinking out loud with an audience than to making a case. Media references in this mode are ambient — they don't need to be connected to an argument to earn their place.

## Content Examples

### Excellent — Personal Reflection
> "I used to think I was an extrovert. Back in uni, I was always the first to suggest a trip to the pub or a game night. Fast forward a few years, add a full-time job, a kid, and a stroke, and suddenly I realised… maybe I just liked having energy."

Why it works: Specific biographical detail. The joke ("maybe I just liked having energy") is tucked inside the reflection, not announced. It earns its weight.

### Excellent — Professional Topic
> "A/B testing in email marketing is like baking a cake. You think you know what works — your audience loves chocolate, right? But without testing, you're just guessing. Testing takes the guesswork out and gives you data to back up your decisions."

Why it works: Analogy opens the idea (step 1), is explained immediately (step 2), translates to principle (step 3), and applies it concretely (step 4). The analogy doesn't float.

### Excellent — RPG / Hobby
> "I still remember the first time I sat behind the GM screen, nervously shuffling my notes. It was a homebrew setting — messy, inconsistent, but full of heart. And they loved it. Well, except for the rogue, who ignored every plan."

Why it works: Specific memory. The wit is inside the closing clause, not announced. The self-deprecation is earned.

Why it works: Concrete description of lived experience with no emotional labelling. "Everything, twice and wonky" earns more than a paragraph of explanation would. The restraint is what gives the last line its weight.

## Pre-Publish Checklist

**Structural flags:**
- [ ] Any "It's not X, it's Y" constructions? → State the argument directly.
- [ ] Exactly three-item lists? → Break the pattern.
- [ ] Passive voice as default? → Flip to active.
- [ ] Every paragraph the same length? → Vary it.
- [ ] Opens immediately with bullet points? → Add framing first.
- [ ] Closing slogan or tagline-style last line? → Let the last thought land as a thought.
- [ ] Is this a post that reads like an article, or an article that reads like a post? → Fix accordingly.
- [ ] Does this end one sentence too late? → Cut the last sentence and reread. If the point already landed, keep the cut.
- [ ] Is the hook a generic topic announcement ("I've been sharing about X" / "My previous posts were about Y")? → Replace with the actual observation or counter-intuitive claim.
- [ ] Is this LinkedIn content being posted to Bluesky? → Adapt for platform or don't post.

**Language flags:**
- [ ] Delve into / deep dive present?
- [ ] "It's important to note" present?
- [ ] "Bear in mind" as opener?
- [ ] "Certainly," as sentence opener?
- [ ] Dashes used where commas or parentheses would work?
- [ ] "Let that sink in" or similar performed pause?
- [ ] Crucial / significant / pivotal as filler?
- [ ] "Underscore" used as a verb?
- [ ] "Serves as" where "is" would do?
- [ ] "Not just X, but also Y" construction?

**Tests:**
- **Qualifiers test**: Remove each modifier. If meaning doesn't change, cut it.
- **Specificity test**: Could this sentence apply to anyone, about anything? If yes, rewrite with names, numbers, or concrete detail.
- **Grace test (personal/faith mode)**: Does this feel lived, or constructed for effect?
- **Structure test (personal mode)**: Does the structure feel planned before the writing started? Formal section headers, argument arcs, and sustained rhetoric in personal writing are often AI-introduced rather than natural. Remove the structure and see if the piece holds without it. Usually it does.
- **Authority test (professional mode)**: Does this sound like someone who knows what they're doing, or someone hoping to be agreed with?
- **Voice test**: Does it sound like Jon talking? If not, that's the problem to solve.

## **Additional Voice Notes (May 2026 Revision)**

### **Uses Ordinary Social Metaphors Rather Than Literary Ones**

Comparisons in Jon’s writing tend to come from familiar lived environments rather than elevated or literary imagery. Menus, pub conversations, inboxes, RPG parties, church life, offices, parenting, or everyday social dynamics appear naturally because they are the environments the voice actually inhabits.

These metaphors usually arrive mid-thought and carry analytical weight without announcing themselves as metaphors.

- “à la carte options off a menu”
- “LLMs don’t think. They predict.”
- “Well, except for the rogue, who ignored every plan.”

The comparison should feel noticed, not crafted.

Avoid:

- Grand or cinematic imagery
- Decorative metaphor detached from lived experience
- Analogies introduced too formally (“It’s like…” unless genuinely natural)

The metaphor is there to clarify the thought, not display cleverness.

---

### **Questions Are Collaborative, Not Performative**

Rhetorical questions in Jon’s voice are usually invitations into shared observation rather than setups for a reveal.

Good:

- “Did you notice what Gill was doing?”
- “What would the psychological repercussions be?”

Less natural:

- “Here’s what Gill was really doing.”
- “Have you ever wondered why…”

The question should assume the reader is capable of arriving at the insight too. The voice is thinking with the audience, not presenting conclusions from above them.

Related pattern:

- Questions are often left partially unanswered.
- The writing trusts the reader to continue the thought.

AI-assisted writing often asks questions only to answer them immediately. Resist this.

---

### **Demonstrated Judgement Over Declared Judgement**

Jon’s writing rarely labels behaviour directly as manipulative, cruel, dishonest, performative, etc. Instead, it describes the framing, wording, or behaviour concretely enough that the judgement emerges naturally.

Good:

“Yet he talks about them as though they’re the à la carte options off a menu.”

Less natural:

“This framing is manipulative and dehumanising.”

The moral or rhetorical weight should emerge from the observation itself. Trust the reader to recognise what they are looking at.

This is especially important in political, faith, and cultural writing. Over-labelling weakens the authority of the point.

---

### **Conversational Pivots Over Formal Transitions**

The writing naturally turns and reorients itself through conversational pivots rather than polished rhetorical sequencing.

Common connective movements:

- “Yet…”
- “Except…”
- “Which is where…”
- “Then suddenly…”
- “And honestly…”
- “Actually…”

These are not structural filler. They create the sense of thought happening in motion.

Avoid:

- “Firstly / secondly / finally”
- “Moreover”
- “Furthermore”
- “This demonstrates that…”
- “One thing worth noting…”

The movement between ideas should feel discovered rather than architected.

---

### **Trusts Adjacency More Than Explanation**

A recurring structural trait in Jon’s writing is placing observations next to one another and trusting the reader to connect them.

Example structure:

- statistic,
- concrete metaphor,
- human consequence.

The connective argument often remains implied.

AI writing tends to over-complete these bridges by explicitly explaining how each point relates to the next. Jon’s voice is usually stronger when some interpretive space remains.

Good:

Statistics about abortion timing followed by the “à la carte menu” metaphor.

Less natural:

“This demonstrates how the framing manipulates emotional perception by…”

The reader should feel trusted, not managed.

---

### **Political and Cultural Writing: Observation Before Argument**

In political or cultural commentary, Jon’s strongest register usually begins with noticing rather than declaring.

Good openings:

- “Did you notice what he was doing?”
- “The strange thing about this argument is…”
- “What struck me watching this…”

Less natural:

- “This is why X is wrong.”
- “Here’s the problem with…”

The writing works best when it sounds like someone thinking carefully in public rather than prosecuting a case.

This does not mean avoiding conviction. It means allowing conviction to emerge through observation, framing, and specificity rather than rhetorical force.
