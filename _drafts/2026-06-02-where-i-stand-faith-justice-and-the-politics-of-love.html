Who I Am

"Christian. Husband. Father. Feminist, increasingly socialist, always anti-fascist. Antipodean immigrant in the UK. Cinephile. Jack-of-all email marketing trades. Board game and RPG enthusiast. Stroke survivor."

You'll see variations of this on my profiles across the social web.

I'm trying to make sense of a world that feels increasingly broken. Where the systems we're told to trust are failing the people they're meant to serve. Where fear and greed are masquerading as governance.

This piece is about faith and politics. The Christian, feminist, increasingly socialist, always anti-fascist parts of that bio. The cinephile shows up through the way I think and express ideas. But you won't find anything about email marketing or games here. Those are other parts of who I am. This isn't about them.

This is about the convictions that shape how I see the world. What I believe needs to change. Particularly for me as a husband and father.

What follows isn't just political opinion. It's rooted in faith. In the words of Jesus. In the conviction that Christianity should look like justice, not power.

“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven... Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled.” (Matthew 5:3, 6)

Jesus didn't preach prosperity gospel. He didn't tell the rich to get richer whilst the poor scraped by. 

The Beatitudes turn worldly power structures upside down. Blessed are the poor. Blessed are those who hunger for justice. Woe to you who are rich.

That's not capitalism. That's not trickle-down economics. That's redistribution. A reordering of priorities where the marginalised matter more than the powerful.

I'm a Christian. Increasingly socialist. Some see a contradiction there. They're looking at a different Jesus than I am. 

When I read the Gospels, I don't see a Jesus who would champion billionaires hoarding wealth whilst people go hungry. I see a Jesus who flipped tables in the temple. Who challenged systems of exploitation. Who spent his time with those society deemed worthless.

"No war but class war." The more I see, the more that resonates. 

The divisions we're told to focus on (left vs right, immigrant vs native, worker vs worker) are distractions. The real divide is between those who own capital and those who have to sell their labour to survive.

“For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.” (1 Timothy 6:10)

Not money itself. The love of it. The hoarding of it. The worship of it.

Right now, a handful of multibillionaires effectively run the world. Politics feels like pantomime. We get to shout, but the script was written before we bought our tickets. The real power lies with those who own capital. The rest of us are just trying to survive within a system designed to extract as much from us as possible.

The UK's wealth inequality tells the story. The number of billionaires has tripled since 1990. Their combined wealth now exceeds £619 billion. Meanwhile, the wealth gap has grown by 50% in just eight years. Most gains going to the top 10% through passive wealth accumulation. Not through work. Just through owning things.

An economy works when money flows. Think of it like water. When it moves, it's clean, drinkable, sustaining life. When it stops, when it pools in one place, it stagnates. Algae grows. Bacteria breeds. It becomes unsafe to drink. You have to treat it before it's useful again.

There's a scene in Snowpiercer where Curtis finally reaches the front of the train. He realises the engine that powers everything is finite. Contained. Running on borrowed time. Literally the labour of children taken from the rear of the train. 

The people at the front convinced themselves they were separate from the tail section. That their luxury wasn't connected to the suffering behind them. But it was the same train. The same closed system. 

That's where we are now. Wealth pooling at the front whilst the rest of the train suffers. Everyone pretending the system can run forever.

In January 2026, the US military removed Venezuela's elected president by force and took control of its oil exports. Cuba (which depended on Venezuelan oil to keep its hospitals running and its lights on) was left with reserves estimated to last weeks. The oil didn't disappear. It was redirected. That's not geopolitics. That's the front of the train deciding who gets fuel.

The super-rich, the 1%, don't have to do anything. They just get wealthier because of how the system works. Digital wealth. Numbers on a screen going up and up. Shares in businesses. Bank accounts that represent more money than they could ever spend. 

And all the while, the rest of us are told the economy is struggling. That we need austerity. That there's not enough to go around.

There is enough. It's just not moving. It's stagnant at the top.

During the Covid pandemic, the government spent taxpayer money. But where did much of it go? Into PPE contracts awarded through a VIP lane to politically connected companies. Into schemes riddled with waste and fraud. 

That's not stimulus. That's daylight robbery dressed up as emergency spending.

In May 2026, Pope Leo XIV published his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas. It addressed artificial intelligence and the concentration of technological power. He named what I'm describing here. The main drivers of development are now private parties with resources exceeding those of governments. "We cannot allow a handful of actors to dictate these processes on their own." He called it the new Tower of Babel: built on pride and the claim to self-sufficiency, without reference to God or neighbour.

I'm not Catholic. But when the head of the Roman Catholic Church releases his first official teaching and it lands on the same diagnosis, that's not nothing.

Capitalism, as we currently practise it, demands infinite growth. But we live on a finite planet. Finite resources. The contradiction feels obvious. We're watching the consequences play out in real time. Capitalism consuming itself whilst the super-rich get richer and the rest of us bear the cost.

“You received without payment; give without payment.” (Matthew 10:8)

The NHS was founded on a socialist principle. Healthcare free at the point of use. Paid for through taxation. Available to everyone regardless of wealth. 

It's not perfect. No system is. But it's rooted in the idea that healthcare is a human right, not a commodity.

For decades, it's been slowly dismantled. In 2011, just 3% of NHS patients were treated by private companies. By 2022, that had jumped to 10%. Outsourcing to private providers has increased consistently. Research links this privatisation to significantly increased rates of treatable mortality.

This isn't efficiency. This is ideology dressed up as pragmatism.

The NHS has been deliberately made inefficient. Red tape and bureaucracy have been added, not streamlined. In 2023 alone, the NHS spent £276 million on locums and outsourcing in radiology. Enough to fund over 2,600 consultant radiologists. 

Instead of investing in permanent staff and infrastructure, the system has been gutted. Creating the perfect conditions to argue for privatisation.

I used to think that people who could afford private health insurance should use it (that it would ease the burden on the NHS). I was wrong. 

Private hospitals don't have their own doctors. They rely on NHS-trained staff. The private sector uses the same finite pool of medical professionals. When you go private, you're paying to skip the queue whilst the system remains strained.

The solution isn't more privatisation. It's investment. Treating healthcare as a public good, not a profit centre. Remembering that the NHS was built on the principle that healthcare should be freely given, not sold to the highest bidder.

“There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear” (1 John 4:18)

Fear is being weaponised. Against immigrants. Against refugees. Against anyone deemed "other." 

Reform, a far-right party, has built its platform on stoking fear of immigration. Nigel Farage (who spent decades campaigning for Brexit whilst benefiting from Europe) now plays the victim when his racist rhetoric is called out. He championed freedom of speech until it was turned on him. 

The irony would be funny if it wasn't so dangerous.

I'm an immigrant. I live here on a visa. If this rhetoric continues unchecked, I'll be affected. So will others who came here, contribute to this country, and do the jobs British people don't want to do.

The numbers don't match the fear. The data doesn't support the narrative that immigration is the root of all problems. But fear doesn't need facts. It just needs repetition and a convenient scapegoat.

Perfect love drives out fear. That's not just a nice verse. It's a command. 

If our faith is rooted in love, we don't fear the immigrant, the refugee, the stranger. We welcome them. We see them as people, not statistics. We recognise that borders are lines drawn by the powerful to control who gets access to resources.

Everything feels like it's sliding towards authoritarianism. Democracy is being hollowed out whilst we're told to be grateful for what little remains. 

I've seen people call for moderation. For "sensible disagreements." For meeting in the middle and respecting each other's opinions.

There's a line in V for Vendetta: "Fear became the ultimate tool of this government." That's what's happening now. Fear of immigrants. Fear of the "other." Fear used to justify surveillance, to erode rights, to consolidate power. 

And it works because people would rather feel safe than free. Until they realise they've lost both.

In June 2025, the US and Israel assassinated Iran's top military leadership, nine nuclear scientists, and eventually its Supreme Leader. Not through war declared by Congress, but through executive action. The script wasn't a warning anymore. It was already written.

But what does that look like when one person's opinion is rooted in falsehoods? When it's based on racism, fascism, bigotry? When it dehumanises the "other" and treats winning as more important than truth?

You can't meet in the middle when the middle keeps shifting. You can't compromise with people who see you as less than human. You can't have a sensible disagreement with someone whose position requires your erasure.

Karl Popper wrote about this: the Paradox of Intolerance. A tolerant society can't tolerate intolerance indefinitely. If we do, intolerance wins and tolerance is destroyed. It's not about being closed-minded. It's about recognising that some positions are incompatible with a free society.

Martin Niemöller wrote: "First they came for the socialists, and I did not speak out – because I was not a socialist."

That poem exists because this pattern repeats. Totalitarianism doesn't announce itself. It creeps in. One compromise at a time. One scapegoat at a time. Until there's no one left to speak out.

“Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs, which on the outside look beautiful but inside are full of the bones of the dead and of all kinds of uncleanness.” (Matthew 23:27)

Jesus reserved his harshest words for religious leaders who performed righteousness whilst exploiting the vulnerable. The Pharisees looked good on the outside. They followed the rules. But their hearts were rotten.

I see the same thing now. People who claim Christianity whilst defending systems that harm the vulnerable. People who invoke faith to justify bigotry, to scapegoat immigrants, to protect the wealthy at the expense of the poor.

That's not Christianity. That's power dressed up as piety.

In the United States, large swathes of Christianity have been co-opted by the authoritarian right. Not recently. For decades. Back through the civil rights movement and before. The Republican Party has been taken over by this strain, and much of American Christianity with it. Twisted into nationalism dressed as faith. Fear weaponised. Love conditional. The "other" demonised.

And then, in March 2026, it stopped being abstract. US military commanders across every branch told their troops that the war on Iran was anointed by Jesus, that Trump had been chosen to light the signal fire for Armageddon. More than 200 complaints were filed. The Pentagon didn't respond to them. It redirected journalists to Pete Hegseth's press conference instead. Hegseth (the Secretary of Defense, with a Jerusalem cross and 'Deus Vult' tattooed on his skin) hosts monthly prayer sessions at the Pentagon featuring pastors who want America to become a Christian theocracy. Power in piety drag. Except now it has nuclear weapons.

The UK isn't immune. After 14 years of Conservative governance (which ended in their worst parliamentary defeat in history) the country faces rising poverty, crumbling infrastructure, and an NHS that's been deliberately undermined. 

We've watched Labour drift so far right it barely resembles the left-leaning movement it once was. The Overton window has shifted. What was once considered centre-ground is now seen as radical.

What baffles me most is watching people defend systems that actively harm them. People who aren't billionaires defending billionaires. People who work for wages defending the erosion of workers' rights. People who benefit from public services voting to dismantle them.

Morpheus was right in The Matrix: the very minds we're trying to reach are still part of the system, which makes them defend it. Not because they benefit from it. Because they can't yet see that they're being used by it. 

Some think they're capitalists because they exchange labour for money. But they're not. They don't own capital that generates wealth. They're workers in a capitalist system, just like the rest of us. 

The only difference is they've been convinced that defending the system will somehow elevate them within it.

It won't. The system isn't designed to lift people up. It's designed to extract.

“Do nothing from selfish ambition or empty conceit, but in humility regard others as better than yourselves. Let each of you look not to your own interests but to the interests of others.” (Philippians 2:3-4)

I've spent this entire piece critiquing systems. Capitalism. Authoritarianism. The co-opting of faith. 

But this isn't abstract. These systems don't just exist "out there." They're in my head. In my habits. In how I was raised and what I still have to unlearn.

I call myself a feminist. Not because I've figured it all out. Because I'm trying to actively dismantle the patriarchy I grew up in. Including the parts of it still lodged in my own thinking.

I was raised with complementarianism. The idea that men and women are "different but complementary." It sounded reasonable at the time. But in practice, it almost always meant men lead and women submit. 

That's not partnership. That's hierarchy with a Christian veneer.

Feminism isn't about elevating women at the expense of men. It's about dismantling the hierarchies that harm everyone. It's egalitarianism in practice. 

And when Paul wrote that in Christ there is neither male nor female, he wasn't talking about biological differences. He was talking about dismantling power structures.

I'm married to an incredible woman. Strong. Career-driven. She gives of herself to everyone around her. She finds real value in her work. I support that absolutely. 

But supporting it intellectually and consistently showing up to share the domestic labour are two different things.

I work from home. I have the time and capacity to do more of the household tasks. And yet, I don't always do them. Sometimes it's forgetfulness. Sometimes it's because my brain doesn't prioritise them the way it should. Sometimes it's because I was raised to see those tasks as someone else's responsibility.

I've learned about weaponised incompetence. The way men "forget" or "don't know how" to do domestic tasks. Forcing women to either do it themselves or manage the labour of teaching and reminding. 

I don't want to be that. But intent doesn't change impact. When I forget, when I don't follow through, the result is the same. More labour falls to my wife.

That's the insidious thing about patriarchy. It's not just a system "out there" that I oppose. It's in my habits. In what I was taught to prioritise and what I was taught to overlook. 

Dismantling it means ongoing, uncomfortable work. Not just in how I think. In how I show up every day.

I'm raising a daughter. I want her to grow up in a world where she's not expected to carry more than her share of invisible labour. Where her value isn't tied to how much she gives of herself. Where partnership actually means partnership.

But that world starts in my own home. In whether I remember to do the washing up without being asked. In whether I notice what needs doing and do it, rather than waiting to be told. In whether I treat domestic labour as shared responsibility, not as help I'm offering.

Feminism isn't just about what I believe, it's about what I do, and I'm still very much learning.

“Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did it to me.” (Matthew 25:40)

This is the heart of it. How we treat the vulnerable, the marginalised, the "least of these" – that's how we treat Jesus.

I don't have all the answers. I don't have a roadmap for fixing this. 

What I do have is a growing clarity about what's wrong. An inability to stomach watching it go unchallenged.

I can see the systems that are broken. I can see the lies we're told to keep us compliant. I can see the fear being weaponised to turn us against each other instead of against the structures that exploit us all.

And I can't stay silent about it.

Pope Leo XIV closed his encyclical with a line from Tolkien. Gandalf, before the battle, to a frightened hobbit: "It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till."

That's the version of hope I can hold. Not that we fix everything. Not that we win. Just that we do what is in us, in the years we're given, in the fields we know.

For myself, my family, my neighbours, this country, and the world:

I want an economy that serves people, not profit. I want politics that lifts people up instead of punching down. I want a faith that looks like Jesus. Challenging. Inclusive. Rooted in justice and love.

I want a world where we stop defending systems that harm us and start building ones that work for everyone. Where billionaires don't run the show. Where workers have power. Where democracy isn't just a word we invoke whilst it's dismantled behind the scenes.

I want a Christianity that stands with the oppressed, not the oppressor. A politics that prioritises humanity over capital. A society where love drives out fear. Where justice isn't a luxury for those who can afford it.

I want my daughter to grow up in a world where she's valued for who she is, not how much she gives.

That's where I stand. For my daughter. For my wife. For every person told they're less than. For the world I want to help build, even if I don't live to see it finished.
