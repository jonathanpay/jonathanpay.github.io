I grew up believing the Bible was one book with one voice.

That’s what I was taught. God’s Word: singular, unified, without error. If two passages seem to contradict, the problem is your reading. If one author says something uncomfortable, keep reading until you find the verse that resolves it. The clear passages interpret the unclear ones.

I held this for years. Shared it with others, built my whole theology on it. Then I started reading the scholarship.

Not apologetics, I’d already received plenty of that already, the historical-critical scholarship. The stuff that doesn’t begin with the assumption that the Bible must be one unified voice and then work backwards to defend it. The stuff that begins with the texts themselves and asks: what’s actually here?

What’s actually here is a library. A historically assembled collection of texts written across roughly a thousand years by dozens of authors (and probably hundreds of editors) in three languages, representing competing theological traditions, using different genres, addressing different communities, answering different questions, and frequently disagreeing with each other. That’s what the Bible is.

The Gospels don’t agree on the day Jesus died. Mark has him crucified on Passover day; John has him crucified the day before. They don’t agree on where Jesus first appeared after the resurrection. Matthew says Galilee, Luke says Jerusalem. They don’t agree on whether Jesus claimed to be God. John’s Jesus says “I and the Father are one,” while Mark’s Jesus won’t even let people call him good: “why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone.” These are different authors making different theological claims, and the canon preserves the difference.

The same thing happens within single books. Genesis opens with two creation accounts (different vocabulary, different sequence, different names for God). The flood narrative has doubled, contradictory details. These are seams where earlier sources have been woven together and the weave is still visible.

Paul’s letters show the same pattern. The Paul of Galatians (the one who says “there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus”) is not the same voice as the Paul of 1 Timothy, who commands women to be silent. The undisputed letters have one voice. The Pastorals have another. 2 Corinthians is probably three or four separate letters stitched into one.

Then there’s the manuscript tradition. Codex Sinaiticus, one of our oldest complete New Testaments, doesn’t match later manuscripts in hundreds of places. John 1:18 reads “unique Son” in some manuscripts and “unique God” in others. The ending of Mark is a later addition. The woman caught in adultery floats between different locations in different manuscripts. The “Johannine Comma” (the only explicit Trinitarian formula in the Bible) was inserted centuries after the fact. None of this is controversial in mainstream scholarship. It’s just what the evidence shows.

And then there’s the canon itself. The 27-book New Testament wasn’t settled at Nicaea. That’s a myth. Marcion proposed a canon first. Athanasius’s Festal Letter of 367 is the first complete list that matches ours. The process took centuries. Books were included, excluded, contested, and re-contested. The canon is the outcome of a long, contested, political process, not the starting point of a settled faith.

Now, I know how this might read: like I’m dismantling the Bible. Like I’m listing all the reasons the Bible can’t be trusted. That’s not so! I’m describing the thing that’s actually there. A library is a different kind of thing from a rulebook. It doesn’t need to speak with one voice to be worth reading. It doesn’t need a single author to be sacred. It doesn’t need internal consistency to be taken seriously.

The problem is what we’ve been taught to expect from it. Inerrancy (the doctrine that the Bible is without error in all it asserts) functions as a harmonisation. When the Bible appears to contradict itself, “inerrancy” demands the contradiction be resolved through harmonisation, contextualisation, or dismissing one text’s plain meaning in favour of another’s. The result is a contemporary composite.

The tradition itself knows this. The church fathers noticed the discrepancies. Augustine wrote an entire book on the harmony of the Gospels because the problem was already visible. The difference is that Augustine believed the harmony was there to be found. The historical-critical reading says the differences are evidence of what the texts actually are: records of developing communities wrestling with God, written by human authors at different moments for different reasons. The reason this matters to me personally is simpler than the scholarship.

I did not leave faith when I stopped reading the Bible as a rulebook. I found a different way of being faithful. The rulebook reading made faith exhausting. Every apparent contradiction was a threat to be managed. Difficult passages were a problem to be solved, and uncomfortable verses had to be contextualised until it stopped being uncomfortable. I spent years doing this, and receiving the same from others. Harmonising, smoothing, explaining away. At some point I realised the thing I was defending wasn’t the Bible. It was my idea of what the Bible had to be in order for my faith to survive.

When I let go of that, something shifted. I stopped needing the Bible to be one voice and started reading it as the library it actually is. The contradictions stopped being threats and started being evidence. The different voices became conversation partners. The difficult passages became readable as what they are: one community’s answer to a question, which another community answered differently. I’d been raised to want a plain blanket. One fabric, one weave, no visible seams. But what I’d actually been given was a quilt. Different pieces, stitched together over centuries by different hands. The variety isn’t a defect, it’s what makes it worth holding onto. That’s an honest faith. And I’d rather have honesty than certainty.

The phrase I keep returning to: the Bible was never designed to do what inerrancy asks of it. It was never one book. It was never written by one author. It was never assembled to be a univocal rulebook. But it is a record, messy and multivocal and historically situated, of people and communities encountering God and trying to make sense of that encounter. Some of what they wrote is beautiful. Some of it is morally difficult. Some of it contradicts other parts. All of it is human. And all of it, taken together, is what we actually have.

Treating the library as a single voice means suppressing what the texts actually do: disagree, develop, and speak from within specific historical moments rather than from an eternal vantage point. Millions of Christians do this, and I understand why. But they’re not reading the Bible at that point. They’re reading a harmonised composite that doesn’t exist in any manuscript or translation.

Admittedly, this post might raise more questions than it answers, and that’s the point.

The question it sets up in my mind (how can you deconstruct and still read Scripture?) is the question I need to answer. The short version: the same way you read any library. Read each voice for what it’s saying. Pay attention to who’s speaking and to whom and why. Let the voices disagree without needing to resolve them into one.
