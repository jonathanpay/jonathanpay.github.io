I never liked street preachers. The ones who set up on a Saturday and shout about Jesus, sin, punishment and forgiveness at people who are trying to get to the shops. It doesn’t sit right with me and it never worked as persuasion. The defence I have heard all my life is that we are sowing seeds, scatter and see what takes, and I used to let that one go. I mind it more each year. There is something in it that treats the person in front of you as ground to be worked, and volume does not make up for standing there with nothing they can use.

I watched people harden against it, not because they had thought their way through anything. Agreeing with a man who is shouting at you feels like surrender, so people talk themselves further into the opposite position to avoid being recruited. Fear recruits for the other side. Those preachers were the loud end of something much quieter that had been handed to me from the inside, that I arrived already condemned. Not because of anything I had done. Guilt was my inheritance, passed down from the first human being, and the only thing between me and an eternity of conscious torment was accepting the correct account of how it had been dealt with.

Which means I was given a God I had to be frightened into loving. Fire and brimstone first, love as the escape hatch, and it should be the other way round. I have known for years that this never sat right without being able to say why.

Adam is adam, and adam is not a name the way Dave is a name. It comes from adamah, the soil: he is made out of the soil and then called Ground, introduced the way you introduce an explanation rather than a person. Genesis 2 and 3 read like an oral account of where we came from, told by people who did not have the vocabulary for any of it. Why do snakes have no legs. Why does childbirth hurt. Why does the ground fight back when you work it. Why do we die. The story answers all four, and the doctrine built on top of it is not in the text, whatever you decide about whether he existed. Genesis 2 and 3 say that humans die, that relationships break, that we are shut out of somewhere we cannot get back to, and that the rest of the book is people trying to get back in. They never say that anybody after Adam is guilty of Adam’s act.

Paul brings it in twice. In Romans 5 he sets Adam and Christ opposite each other as two representatives, one whose act brought death to everyone and one whose act brings life, and in 1 Corinthians 15 he calls Christ the last Adam. Dan McClellan and Dan Beecher read it as a counterweight to Christ rather than a report of anything that happened. I cannot tell you what Paul thought Genesis was, because that is inside his head and we have no access to it. What I can tell you is what he does with the text. He takes a story he does not appear in and gives its central figure a headship that reaches everyone who came after him. The translation trouble sits on Romans 5:12, where Pete Enns points out that the reading turns on the Latin, in quo omnes peccaverunt, in whom all sinned, and the Greek more naturally reads because all sinned, which puts the act with each person. It does not sit on 5:18, where one man’s trespass leads to condemnation for all, or on 1 Corinthians 15:22, where in Adam all die, and no argument about a preposition dissolves those. Paul thought Adam’s act reached everyone. What he does not say is that anyone was guilty before doing anything themselves.

The phrase original sin does not turn up until Augustine, and neither does the thing the phrase names in its finished form. The earlier fathers had death and a weakened will. Justin Martyr, in the middle of the second century, ties sin to death. Tertullian, fifty years on, articulates an inherited sin and in the same breath argues against hurrying infants to baptism. Irenaeus has Adam’s disobedience undone by Christ’s obedience with nothing handed down in between. Origen reads the church’s practice back onto the newborn. Cyprian gets the African bishops to rule on it at Carthage in 253. What none of them has is an infant who arrives condemned. The doctrine leans on a second verse just as hard, and Psalm 51:5 is one man’s line about his own conception that the tradition turned into a statement about everyone. Adam is not named anywhere in the psalm.

Then the Latin does the rest. Augustine reads the in whom of Romans 5:12 as Adam, which puts all of us there with him, so the act is counted as ours and the damage is underneath us before we have done anything. Now the infant is not only sick, but condemned, and the condemnation travels in the body. Bart Ehrman, working the same history on his own podcast this year, adds the detail I was never told about: Augustine held that the condition was passed on through semen, in the act itself, so the guilt travelled in the thing I had spent years being taught to be ashamed of. The doctrine and the shame are one mechanism rather than two that happen to sit together.

He built that in an argument with a man called Pelagius, a British monk who read Paul as saying Adam harmed himself and set a bad example, and that everyone else becomes a sinner through their own choices. Augustine read the Latin as saying all of us were there in Adam, so all of us are born guilty, and Augustine won. Pelagius was acquitted at a synod in Palestine in 415, and the bishop of Rome read his confession as orthodox before reversing under pressure from the African bishops and the emperor. Carthage condemned him in 418, and the emperor’s weight is what made the condemnation hold. Julian of Eclanum carried the argument on and accused Augustine of never having stopped being a Manichaean, which scholars still argue about. The East never took the guilt at all, keeping ancestral sin, and the Reformed tradition later pushed what was left into total depravity, which is a different claim again. That is how a doctrine gets settled.

Genesis 3 is never picked up again in the Hebrew Bible, and never as an explanation for anyone else’s guilt. It is not on the lips of Jesus, who quotes Genesis on marriage from the first two chapters, and Adam is named once in the Gospels, in a genealogy in Luke. In the whole New Testament the story surfaces in Paul, and in a letter written in his name after he was dead.

Two places you can go to argue against me. Hosea 6:7 has Israel transgressing the covenant like Adam, and Job 31:33 has Job protesting that he has not covered his transgression like Adam. The Hebrew there is ke’adam, which can be read as like Adam or simply as like any man, and in Hosea some translators have taken it as a place name instead. Take the reading the doctrine needs and see what it does with him. Hosea says Israel broke the covenant the way Adam did. Job says he has not done what Adam did, which is hide. That is typology, and typology is the bridge Paul crosses, so the Hebrew Bible hands the later doctrine its method and never its content. Two references and no inheritance is a thin thread for something I was told everything hung on.

The Hebrew Bible had already argued this out internally, which is the part I find hardest to get round. Ezekiel 18 and Jeremiah 31 both quote the same proverb, that the fathers have eaten sour grapes and the children’s teeth are set on edge, and both bring it up in order to overrule it. That proverb is the inherited-guilt position, and it is in the text so the prophets can put it down. The other side is in there too, in the commandment about visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation. Paul is a Pharisee before he is anything else and he is reading those scriptures, renegotiating a text that already contains the argument he is making and landing on the side the prophets argued against. Put Ezekiel 18 beside Romans 5 and the two do not sit easily together. I lean towards Ezekiel, and I would not pretend that is more than a lean.

The original sin is neither original nor sin.[1] The reason this is not academic for me is that I was handed it and then I handed it on. I sat in rooms and told people they were born condemned, and I meant it as care. Some of them are still carrying it.

So if there is no inheritance, what is sin? I am still working that out, but I know where I would start. Jesus got asked what the whole thing comes down to, and he said love God and love your neighbour as yourself, and the law and the prophets hang on that. He did not say anything about Adam. So I measure it by the neighbour: where I do not love the people in front of me, where I take from them or walk past them, that is the ground I would call sin. A thing I do, and keep doing. A pattern we participate in, and participation can be stopped. I should be honest that the scholarship complicates this. McClellan argues the Bible’s sin language is mostly about ritual purity and contamination rather than morality, and that sin is a category with no fixed definition. I am happy to lean into that, but it is not something most people can pick up and use, and I am writing for people who need something they can hold. So I am holding the moral argument, and being plain that I am doing constructive work with the word rather than reporting how the Bible uses it. Most people just will not admit that.

There is a version of sin is where you fail to love that turns into a machine for destroying yourself, and that is not the one I mean. Being kind has a limit, and I have come to call that limit stewardship. There was a stretch when a stroke, a new baby, a move across the country and a pandemic all landed inside about two years, and I could not be what was being asked of me. Extending myself past what I had did not produce kindness. It produced a person with nothing left to give. You cannot pour out of an empty jug.

Sin and salvation are two sides of the same coin, so what I have said about one has said something about the other. If sin is not a verdict I was born into, there is no inherited debt that has to be paid off on my behalf, and the cross stops being a punishment. I read the cross as solidarity now: God entering the worst thing human beings do to each other and refusing to answer it with force. It also means a God who does not coerce has no reason to close the door on anyone, and I am inclined to think the door does not close. I will come back to that properly another time.

I have not seen one of those preachers in a while, which makes it easier to say this than it would be standing in front of one. I do not resent him. He is passing on something he was handed, which is what I did too, and I would rather ask him about Genesis 3 than shout back across the road.

  1. Dan McClellan’s line, from the Data Over Dogma episode Not Original, Not Sin (22 December 2025). The footnote-as-a-bit is his too, borrowed from The Bible Says So, where the chapter titles run on pop lyrics and carry a footnote apologising to whoever wrote them.
