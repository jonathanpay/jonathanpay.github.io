I grew up believing the Bible had a clear sexual ethic. One man + one woman = one marriage, to the exclusion of all others. The verses were right there, the framework was obvious. I built my understanding around it and lived it. I don’t believe that anymore. Not because I stopped reading the Bible, but because I started reading it more carefully, with better tools and different views than the ones I’d been given. The sexual ethic the church (at large) claims to derive from scripture rests on foundations that don’t survive contact with the evidence of scholarship with mainstream consensus.

Take adultery. In the ancient Near East, it was a crime against a husband’s property rights in his wife’s sexuality. A married woman’s body belonged to her husband, another man sleeping with her was theft. You can watch the logic operate in the Decalogue itself: the tenth commandment lists a neighbour’s wife alongside his male and female slaves, his ox, and his donkey. She is an item in an inventory. The church spiritualised the seventh commandment into a universal prohibition on extramarital sex, and while the moral intuition behind that isn’t wrong, the underlying logic of the text is ownership. If you’re building a sexual ethic on “what the Bible says about adultery,” you need to know it wasn’t talking about consent.

The canon knows this, incidentally, and revises it. In Mark, Jesus says a man who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her. Two words, and the wrong has moved. In a legal system where adultery was an offence against the other husband, Jesus makes it an offence against the woman. That is the same move I am making in this post, made first inside the text, roughly nineteen centuries earlier.

Same with marriage. The bride’s father and the groom made the agreement, the bride was the object transferred. The commonest Hebrew verb rendered “to marry” is laqach, which means, plainly, to take. It’s the verb used when God tells Abraham to take his son Isaac up the mountain, and when Shechem takes Dinah. Polygyny was assumed (Abraham, Jacob, David, Solomon, none of them condemned, just described). Concubinage was regulated, not prohibited. Levirate marriage required a man to marry his dead brother’s widow regardless of his own preferences. Deuteronomy provides a procedure for taking a woman captured in war as a wife, and nobody asks her. The modern idea of marriage, freely chosen and monogamous and (more or less) egalitarian, is a development the tradition arrived at over centuries. A good one, but claiming it as “what the Bible teaches” means ignoring what the Bible actually describes.

The LGBTQ prooftexts are the clearest case of reading modern questions into ancient texts. Leviticus, Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 6, 1 Timothy 1: these were identity-maintenance boundary markers for a minority community. The ancient world had no concept of sexual orientation as an innate feature of personhood. The biblical authors were not addressing whether two people of the same sex could form a loving, covenantal relationship. That question wasn’t live in their world, and reading these texts as if they answer it is like reading a first-century medical text and expecting it to distinguish between bacterial and viral infections. The categories weren’t there.

The purity laws in Leviticus were identity markers. They served to distinguish Israel from surrounding nations: what you eat, what you wear, who you sleep with, all of it performing “we are not them.” The early church itself recognised this with the food laws, boldly abandoning kosher in Acts 10 and Galatians 2 while keeping the sex laws. The distinction between “ceremonial” and “moral” law that’s used to justify that selectivity was constructed later. The texts don’t make it. The tradition did, then claimed the texts had always done so. If you’re going to keep Leviticus 18 while discarding Leviticus 11, you need a better reason than “one feels moral and the other doesn’t,” because that feeling is itself a product of the tradition you’re trying to defend with it.

Gender hierarchy was the cultural water every biblical author swam in. Female subordination was assumed as natural (the ancient biological view literally understood women as imperfect men) and the household codes in Ephesians, Colossians, and 1 Peter reflect standard patriarchal structures without a hint that the authors saw anything strange about them. The complementarian claim that these establish timeless “biblical manhood and womanhood” ignores that the same codes also assume slavery as part of the household order. The church eventually abandoned that bit, after centuries of resistance and a civil war. The question is whether what the texts say about gender is culturally contingent or divinely mandated, and the evidence points firmly to the former. The church changed its mind on slavery. It can change its mind here too.

And the Bible’s own voices disagree on much of this. The same Paul who wrote “there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus” also appears in 1 Timothy commanding women’s silence and submission (well, “Pauls”, but more on that another time). Divorce is the clearest example, because you can watch the position move across a single generation. Mark prohibits it outright, no exceptions, both remarrying parties commit adultery. Matthew adds an escape clause for porneia. Paul, writing earlier than either, adds a different one: if an unbelieving spouse walks out, the believer is not bound. Three texts, three positions, and the canon keeps all three. These aren’t the same voice making the same point with slightly different emphasis. They’re different voices making different claims at different moments for different communities, and the canon preserves the difference.

So if the ethic I was taught doesn’t come from the texts, where does it come from? This is the part I found genuinely disorienting, because the answer is much more recent than I expected.

Paul’s own preference was celibacy. He says so repeatedly in 1 Corinthians 7, and he isn’t coy about it. Marriage is the concession for people who can’t manage the alternative, better to marry than to burn, and the whole thing is framed by the conviction that the appointed time has grown short and the present form of this world is passing away. He is not writing a sexual ethic for the next two thousand years. He is advising people who expect the world to end within their lifetimes. Medieval Catholicism, whatever else you make of it, read him correctly: celibacy was the higher calling, marriage the legitimate lesser one, and by the eleventh century priests’ wives were doing penance and their children were being declared illegitimate.

Then the Reformers inverted it. Luther and Calvin married as theological statements, clerical celibacy requirements ended, marriage was redeclared a natural state. The high Protestant theology of marriage, marriage as the image of Christ and the church, the family as the basic unit of Christian society, was constructed after that decision, not before it. Luther, incidentally, said he found nothing in scripture prohibiting polygamy, which is not a sentence you hear quoted often in the churches that claim him. The Victorians added the domestic ideal and the pure wife. The 1990s added saving yourself. That is a five-hundred-year construction project, and my youth group was standing at the end of it, being told it went back to Eden.

There’s a smaller thing tucked inside this that I can’t unsee. If marriage is the concession offered to those who burn, it is offered to exactly one orientation. Everyone else gets told to try harder at the thing Paul admitted most people can’t do. Either the concession is universal or the framework is discriminatory in application while claiming to be universal in principle.

Across four disciplines, four books I’ve read in this last year make the same structural move: the received narrative about human sexuality doesn’t survive contact with the evidence. Nadia Bolz-Weber (theology): the body is sacred. Emily Nagoski (neuroscience): desire is a dual-control system. Christopher Ryan and Cacilda Jethá (anthropology): monogamy is a recent cultural construction. Janet Hardy and Dossie Easton (ethics): consent is active collaboration. Four disciplines, one finding. The standard narrative is wrong.

Now, I should say what this isn’t, because I know how a post like this reads to someone who was taught the version of Christianity I was taught. It reads like an attack, like I’m saying the Bible is useless and sexual ethics don’t matter. That’s not so!

I’m saying the sexual ethic the church claims to derive from the Bible is a historical construction, built from property law and boundary markers and gender assumptions the tradition itself has already partially abandoned, and then assembled into its current shape by people whose names we know, in a century we can date. The question is whether to build on foundations that actually survive scrutiny.

The constructive half of my project is about rebuilding from better ones: a theological anthropology that treats bodies as good, an account of love the tradition’s own highest theology already supplies (non-possessive, self-giving, kenotic) and a sexual ethic grounded in consent and mutuality and the conviction that love participates in the life of a God whose own love doesn’t coerce.

The reason this matters goes deeper than the evidence I’ve just walked through, and it changes how you read the whole story.

The picture we’re given in Genesis 1 and 2 is of a garden where Adam has need of nothing. No planting, no harvesting, no owning. Then something changes. Suddenly there are clothes fashioned from animal hides. Suddenly there is shame about the body. Suddenly the ground is cursed and work produces sweat. The traditional reading says this is about sin, disobedience, pride, the rejection of God’s authority. But there is another reading: the Fall is a memory of the agricultural transition. The serpent didn’t introduce sex into the garden. The serpent introduced ownership.

Christopher Ryan and Cacilda Jethá, in Sex at Dawn, argue that patriarchal monogamy is a recent development, an adaptation to settled agriculture, private property, and the need for paternity certainty once land started being passed down through male heirs. Hunter-gatherer societies, for the vast majority of human history, organised sex and kinship differently. Not chaotically, there were structures and norms, but the structures weren’t built on ownership. Then came agriculture. Suddenly there was surplus. Suddenly there was land that belonged to someone. Suddenly women’s bodies needed to be controlled, because if you were going to pass property down a male line, you needed to know whose child was whose. And for the vast majority of humans in all of recorded history, existence remained hand-to-mouth. The surplus went up, not down.

The church spiritualised the Fall into a guilt story about sex and bodies. Perhaps, instead, the fall was into property and inequality. If the Fall wasn’t about sex, then the shame that purity culture has built on top of the Eden story falls away itself.
