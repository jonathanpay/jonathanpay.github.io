This post stays pinned at the top. It's less a post than a statement of intent, and I'll come back and update it between now and 40.

I turn 40 in about seven months. I've travelled for work and for pleasure, and spent holidays with family in different countries. Most people who know me only know about one of the things I've done.

That's normal. People get known for one thing at a time. Even if they've done different things at different stages of their life, that's usually what sticks, for celebrities and for the rest of us with smaller audiences.

When someone known for one thing starts talking about something else, it gets called a pivot. A rebrand. Sometimes a betrayal, as if they'd been one thing all along and suddenly became another. I don't think that's what happens. I think most people were several things the whole time, and only one of them was getting attention.

Not a sports car

In some ways, this is my midlife crisis.

Not the version the media has sold for generations. No sports car. No significantly younger girlfriend.

Not even necessarily a crisis. That's just what this stage of life has always been called.

I'm a Christian whose marriage is ending, in a tradition that has plenty to say about that. We talked about it together before either of us talked about it anywhere else. It's hard. We think we'll both be better off for it.

I recently came across a TikTok account run by a man who's 42, going through a divorce, living alone in a one-bedroom flat. Every video, he picks one thing and talks about it. The whole account is his midlife reset.

That sounds remarkably like me. What he's doing is good, admirable even, and I see a lot of myself in him. I've thought about doing something similar.

I haven't, and part of the reason is this post. That account is one thing, done well. It's not his whole personality, and I don't want it to become mine.

Mine, not modelled

Every platform I've joined has got a version of me, shaped by what the platform's for and who's watching. Twitter had my faith loudly, until I noticed out loud in 2016 that I'd mostly stopped tweeting Christian things because the account had become work-networking. Instagram gets church at Christmas. Pixelfed gets a scripture verse with a caption under it, maybe the most honest format I've found for talking about faith in public. None of that's lying. It's just what showing up somewhere does to you.

I've known the shape of this for twenty years or more: if you're not paying for it, you're the product. What I hadn't done, until I exported my own data to see what these platforms had actually inferred about me, was watch it happen in practice rather than just know the slogan. LinkedIn's data export contains a buyer profile built from 904 "skills" it thinks it can sell me under, the same 2012 graduation year it's carried untouched ever since, and no actual person in it anywhere. Twitter's version of me is male, somewhere between thirteen and fifty-four, interested in football and shopping. I'll take a wrong guess about football over a right one about anything else. That's not a worse copy of me. It's a different person, wearing my name, built out of what I clicked.

This site doesn't have that problem. Nobody's selling anything here but my own say in it. It's the one thing I've built that isn't shaped by somebody else's model of what I'm worth showing, which is probably why it's got room for a post like this one.

The version of me it carries isn't fixed either. The bio I use everywhere: Christian. Husband. Father. Feminist, increasingly socialist, always anti-fascist. Antipodean immigrant in the UK. Cinephile. Jack-of-all email marketing trades. Board game and RPG enthusiast. Stroke survivor. At some point "husband" becomes "ex-husband." "Father" stays, and gets a word added alongside it: co-parent, because that's what we're doing. Neither word is the whole of me, but they're true, or about to be, and I'd rather say so here, on the thing I own, than leave it unsaid. My kids didn't sign up for a blog, so that's as far as that goes.

Paul has a passage in 1 Corinthians about the body having many parts. The eye can't say to the hand, "I don't need you." He's talking about the church, not one person, and I won't stretch it further than it goes. But I keep coming back to it. A body that only shows off its hands is still a whole body. It's just hiding most of itself.

The bits that rub

Not all of these parts sit comfortably next to each other. I'd be lying if I said they did.

My faith shapes my politics, and my politics aren't what a lot of Christians would expect. Writing openly about either can cost you work in an industry where the safe move is to say nothing that isn't about open rates. I work with my mum, so my reputation and hers are tangled together. I don't get to pretend my choices only land on me. Some clients will read a piece on Christian nationalism and quietly decide I'm not for them. That's their call to make.

The tensions run the other way too. I read the Bible as something other than a rulebook, which puts me at odds with some of the people I grew up in church with. I'm a feminist inside a tradition that has spent a long time arguing about whether women should be allowed to preach. And I spend my working days helping brands sell things, while writing here about a Jesus who had sharp words for the rich.

I don't have a tidy answer for any of that. I'm not sure I want one yet. What I want is to stop editing myself down into a single, marketable shape and see what happens when I don't.

Being more of myself in public doesn't mean being all of myself in public. Some parts of life stay where they are.

The thesis

So here's the project for the months between now and 40.

Write about more of it. Not equally, and not to a schedule, but enough that this site reads like a person with a job, a faith, a family, and a shelf full of board games.

In practice, that looks like:

  • Giving Life and Fun regular posts, not occasional ones. Films, games, travel, the stroke, being Australian in England.
  • Keeping the Faith writing going, and no longer treating it as the awkward bit at the bottom of the page.
  • Doing good work, and letting it sit next to everything else without apologising for either.
  • Writing about the places where these things rub against each other, instead of keeping them in separate rooms.

I'll come back to this post as the months go on and add what's worked, what hasn't, and what I've changed my mind about. If I've learnt anything in 39 years, it's that I'll change my mind about something.

If you came here for email, the email's still here. If you came for the faith writing, so is that. If you stick around for the rest, I'd like that.
