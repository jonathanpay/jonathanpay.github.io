I know it’s an image of roots and not weeds – forgive me!

About a year ago I wrote a piece called The Rotten Fruit of Christian Nationalism. It came out of a Facebook post I’d written after Charlie Kirk was killed, and a conversation with an old friend that followed. This September was the anniversary of Kirk’s death. My friend posted about it, and the same conversation started up again.

It went differently this time. Some of that was good. Some of it wasn’t, so I’ll start there.

Hands up, I goaded him. Nothing cruel, but I saw the post and the comments under it and I went in with an edge. Someone else in the thread had shared a list of reasons they thought Kirk wasn’t a good guy. My friend called it soundbites taken out of context, and instead of asking him what he meant, I pushed. I knew what I was doing. Paul says in Galatians, “Let us not become conceited, provoking and envying each other.” That’s what I was doing. Provoking.

The reason I went in at all is that I’m no longer okay with staying silent. If I see something being said or done that I think is wrong, and I’m able to say something, I will. For years I didn’t. I told myself I didn’t know enough, or that this kind of thing was attention-seeking and would go away if sensible people ignored it. It didn’t go away. It got bigger. I used to call my silence humility. Really I was just avoiding it.

So I spoke up. And then I got lost in the weeds.

The weeds

Over the next few weeks I fact-checked almost everything. Every claim on the list, every response my friend gave, every source he sent. Some of it held up and some of it didn’t. In a few places the list was unfair to Kirk, and I said so. In one place I was flatly wrong. I’d told my friend a scandal he mentioned didn’t exist, and it did. (It was about air traffic controllers, not the airline pilots Kirk had been talking about, but I’d still said it wasn’t there.) I owned that in the thread. By the end I’d conceded a few more things too, on how to read a couple of passages of scripture, and on what to call the killing itself. Every one of those concessions made the conversation better.

At one point my friend called the list a Gish Gallop. That’s when someone throws out so many claims so quickly that nobody can answer them all, and hopes some of it sticks. The name comes from Duane Gish, a creationist who used to do it in live debates against scientists, where his opponents only had a few minutes to respond. There’s a related idea called Brandolini’s Law: it takes far more effort to disprove a claim than to make one.

I watched Brandolini’s Law happen in real time. My friend sent me a clip of Kirk explaining his views on the Civil Rights Act. In a few seconds, Kirk got two legal points wrong, the law behind a voter ID lawsuit and what a Supreme Court ruling had actually decided. It took me a fair bit of digging to untangle them.

But a Facebook thread runs on a different clock to a live debate. We had days between replies. We could check things, and we did. And most of the list held up when we checked it, which is pretty much the opposite of a Gish Gallop.

The problem was that I got so caught up in checking each claim that I forgot what the original question was. Someone said they didn’t think Kirk was a good guy. My friend asked why. The list was the answer. It was meant to show a pattern, and I spent weeks arguing about individual pieces of it instead.

The odd thing is that I’d already learned this. A year ago, in the comments under my first post, someone told me the clips were out of context. I wrote back that we could keep dissecting each one, but the wider pattern was clear to me. This year a friend said the same thing about soundbites, and I went straight back to dissecting.

The bigger picture

Words have meaning, and that’s what we spent most of our time arguing about. But words also have an effect, and that adds up over time. You can explain away almost any single sentence if you add enough context. It’s much harder to explain away years of someone’s public speech, and what it did to real people.

Take Martin Luther King. My friend and I went back and forth about King’s private life, his affairs, the FBI files. None of it touched the actual question. Kirk called King “awful” at the same event where he called the Civil Rights Act “a huge mistake.” He softened both later. But taken together, they point one way, and I’d spent my energy arguing about whether each one had been clipped fairly.

Last year I said I wanted to come back to the idea of a “marketplace of ideas,” and whether it still works when the loudest and most divisive voices get the most amplification. This conversation gave me part of an answer. Even with weeks to check things, and two people mostly trying to argue in good faith, the quick claim still had the advantage. Turning Point was bringing in around $85 million a year, much of it spent making one voice as loud as possible. That’s not a marketplace where the best idea wins.

Jesus said, “By their fruit you will recognise them.” You don’t judge a tree by picking over every leaf. You look at what it produces.

Arguing as Christians

My friend and I are both Christians. We read the same Bible and come to very different conclusions about a lot of it. I hold more loosely than he does to the doctrines that built up since the Bible was written, and we read Jesus differently on sex, gender, and marriage. I don’t expect a Facebook thread to change that, and I don’t think it has to for the conversation to be worth having. If we went back to the Beatitudes, I’d hope we’d find ourselves much closer together.

Some of what we argued about isn’t abstract for me. I’ve got to know trans people, shared hobbies with them, and sat in church with them. Books have their place. But “love your neighbour” means knowing your neighbour first.

What I want to get better at is how I have the conversation. Ephesians talks about “speaking the truth in love.” I’ve mostly heard that used as a reason to soften things. I’ve come to read it as saying the hard thing clearly, in a way that doesn’t push the other person away. Checking myself before I reply. Saying so when I’m wrong. Not goading people just because I can. And still saying what I actually came to say.

I’ve had to be honest about something else, too. A year ago I wrote that Kirk got exactly what he called for. I wrote it in anger. I still think there’s truth in it, in that the climate he helped build came back on him. But it wasn’t just, and he didn’t deserve to be shot. Those two things sit uncomfortably together, and I’d rather hold both than pretend one of them away.

I didn’t celebrate his death. I don’t celebrate anyone’s death. But I didn’t mourn him the way I would someone else, either. I’m still working out what that says about me, and I’d rather admit it than pretend I felt something I didn’t.

What I do know is that I’d rather speak up and get it a bit wrong, and learn from that, than stay silent and tell myself it’s wisdom. If you’ve found a better way to argue with people you care about, about things that matter, I’d really like to hear it.
