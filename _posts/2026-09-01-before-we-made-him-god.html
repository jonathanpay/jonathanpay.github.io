For most of my life I assumed Jesus knew he was God, the way you know your own name: fully aware, from somewhere in his human mind, that he was the second person of the Trinity walking around Galilee. The earliest evidence tells a different story.

Jesus was a Jew arguing with other Jews. His disagreements with the Pharisees were arguments within Judaism. Pete Enns’s Bible for Normal People podcast has covered this a lot: the sharp line between “Judaism” and “Christianity” only gets drawn after 70 CE, once the Temple is gone and rabbinic Judaism and the Jesus movement are working out what comes next as siblings. “Messiah” meant an anointed role. Kings were anointed. So were priests. The Dead Sea Scrolls describe several different messianic figures, some priestly, some royal. Whatever people came to believe about Jesus after his death, nobody was checking him against an existing list.

The Gospels are theological narratives more than neutral reports. Mark writes from the far side of the resurrection, and the “messianic secret” (Jesus telling people to keep quiet about who he is) reads like a device that lets the reader know more than the characters do. Older methods for reconstructing “the real Jesus underneath the Gospels” assumed you could cleanly separate authentic material from invented material. Scholars have mostly abandoned that assumption. What’s left is pattern rather than certainty: teaching in parables, an association with John the Baptist, a kingdom announced in the present tense, table fellowship with people he wasn’t supposed to eat with, and an execution by the state. That’s a thin biography by modern standards. It’s also, for ancient history, a fairly solid one.

Then there’s the resurrection, where historians have to be honest about the limits of the method. Paul, writing within a few years of the crucifixion and quoting a creed older than his own letter, never mentions an empty tomb. His entire case rests on people, starting with himself, claiming to have seen Jesus alive. Bart Ehrman, who built a career on this question and started out an apologist arguing the opposite, now says neither the honourable burial nor the empty tomb clears the historian’s bar. A Sanhedrin member securing dignified burial for a man Rome had just executed for insurrection doesn’t fit what’s known about how Rome treated crucified bodies. What clears the bar is smaller and stranger: something happened that convinced a scattered, frightened group of people that Jesus had been raised.

Now, I know how that reads to anyone taught the resurrection is the whole ballgame, the one fact everything else stands or falls on. That’s not so! People having experiences they interpreted as the risen Jesus is one of the best-attested things about early Christianity there is. What’s thin is everything popular apologetics stacks on top of it. The empty tomb was never part of the core, minimal case scholars actually agree on. The story of cowardly disciples suddenly emboldened assumes a version of events the Gospels don’t agree on: Matthew sends them to Galilee for the appearance, Luke has it happen in Jerusalem the same day, with Jesus telling everyone to stay put. Even Matthew has some, maybe all, of the eleven still doubting while they’re looking straight at him. Paul is clear the risen body stayed physical: his word for it, pneuma, meant a refined but still material substance to his readers. Even at its highest point, the tradition couldn’t quite let go of the body.

From there the Christology climbs, and you can watch it happening inside the New Testament itself. Mark’s Jesus deflects being called good and claims a kind of priestly authority. One early manuscript of Luke has God say “today I have begotten you” at the baptism rather than “with you I am well pleased,” placing Jesus becoming God’s son at a specific moment. Paul talks about Jesus exalted at the resurrection. Only John has Jesus making the explicit claims, “I and the Father are one,” “before Abraham was, I am,” with no parallel anywhere in the other three Gospels. That reads like decades of theological reflection placed back into Jesus’s own mouth.

John’s Gospel gets closest, and even it stops short. Dan McClellan reads the grammar in John 1 like this: “the Word was god” uses the form Greek writers reached for when naming a divine but subordinate figure, the same category ancient Jewish thought already had a slot for, an angel or agent who carries God’s name and authority without being identical to God. The highest Christology in the New Testament canon is a ladder. The manuscripts carry the argument forward. A single Greek letter decides whether John calls Jesus “the unique Son” or “the unique God.” The verse that states the Trinity outright, the Johannine Comma added to 1 John 5, doesn’t appear in any Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century. Someone added it because the doctrine needed a proof text and didn’t have one.

The whole thing gets forced into a decision at Nicaea in 325. Arius argued the Son was created, reading a set of texts that do subordinate the Son to the Father. He lost because Constantine backed the other side and had him exiled. Homoousios survived because it had an empire behind it. Most historians doubt it would have lasted long without one.

Underneath all of this sits an older, stranger question I’m not going to resolve here. The Hebrew Bible has its own heavenly court: the sons of God, the divine council Yahweh presides over in places like Psalm 82, subordinate divine beings demoted over the centuries into angels, then into whatever category John’s “I am” is drawing on, then flattened further into the single Trinitarian godhead the councils enforced. What’s actually at stake theologically is whether that specific, historically compressed picture we call the Trinity is the one that’s real. That’s a stranger question than the one usually being asked. It deserves its own post.

There’s a framing close to C.S. Lewis’s that I keep coming back to: if none of this is real, arguing over the fine print doesn’t matter much either way; if it is real, in whatever form turns out true, this is about the most important thing there is. I’m not going to pretend I can settle which.

What the historical work lets me do is separate two things I used to hold as one package. There’s the mundane layer: an itinerant Jewish teacher who opposed empire and ate with the wrong people, telling anyone who’d listen to love God and love their neighbour as themselves. Read historically, that’s a programme: table fellowship as a political act, a kingdom announced in the present tense rather than promised for later. That layer doesn’t wait on anything else being settled. It’s worth living by whatever eventually gets decided about the resurrection or the Trinity. Then there’s the layer built on top to explain what all of that means cosmically, and that’s where my confidence has dropped. I was taught penal substitutionary atonement and original sin were the load-bearing floor. On the evidence, they look like later constructions instead. I’m holding them now as open questions.

Rather than work that out from scratch, I’d rather find where the work’s already been done, and it mostly has. Liberation theology got there first. James Cone read Jesus’s execution as siding with the oppressed against Rome. Adam Clark, a theologian Cone mentored, describes that tradition as recovering the whole person, body and soul together, against a church that had gone disembodied and abstract. It’s the same move applied to Christology and atonement instead of marriage: keep what was load-bearing, question what got added later to explain it. I’ve mostly met this material secondhand so far, through other people’s summaries rather than reading Cone directly. That’s a gap worth closing.

The reason any of this matters to me personally is simpler than the scholarship makes it sound. For the best part of a decade I was trained to defend a very specific package: a Jesus fully aware of his own divinity from the start, a theology of the cross settled in advance. I didn’t just believe it. I taught it to other people in small groups and on volunteer teams with the same confidence it had been given to me, and loosening my grip on the metaphysics hasn’t loosened my grip on the man.

My favourite verse outside the Gospels is 1 Samuel 14, where Jonathan and his armour-bearer climb up to a Philistine outpost on nothing more than “it may be that God is with us.” Willingness. They go up, a commotion breaks out, and the battle turns. Loving my neighbour and staying suspicious of empire doesn’t need a settled Christology any more than Jonathan needed a settled theology of divine action before he climbed. The historical Jesus, the one you can actually trace evidence for, models that kind of faith far better than the Nicene Christ ever could: grounded and active, willing to be wrong about the metaphysics and climb the cliff anyway.

I don’t know yet what to do with the divine council thread, or the Trinity question underneath it. But I know what to do with a teacher who fed people, argued with power, and got killed for it. That part I can act on today.
