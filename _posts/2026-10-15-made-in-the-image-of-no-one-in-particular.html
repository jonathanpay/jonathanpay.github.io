If you’ve spent any time on LinkedIn, do you ever stop scrolling and think:

haven’t I read this before?

You have, in a way, not that exact post but something that sounds like it. Uses the same rhythms, wraps up with a thought that feels designed to “land” rather than conclude, and you realise you’ve read the shape of it a dozen times without being able to say whose it was. The individual voice is somewhere underneath all that, probably, but you can’t quite hear it.

Stand outside on a quiet morning and you might hear birdsong. Then the crows arrive, one at first, then a few more, because crows are social and loud and when the conditions suit them they multiply. What was varied and interesting birdsong flattens into a single relentless uniformity. The individual voices aren’t gone. They’re just impossible to hear.

I wrote about it on my professional blog in March, and gave the whole thing a name: CAWing, Computer-Aided Writing. I use these tools myself, and I try to use them well, though I know people who would say that’s a contradiction. A language model doesn’t think. It predicts, producing the next most statistically likely word given everything it has been trained on, and the most likely next word is the one that has already appeared most often. Point enough people at the same tool for the same purpose and the output converges on the average of what already exists, and what you get is a flattening: posts that could have come from anywhere and feel like they came from nowhere. Nobody has to intend any of it.

All it takes is conditions that reward frequency. Post more, stay visible, keep up. When keeping up is the goal, the tool that makes it easier is the one you reach for.

What I didn’t get into in that post, because it wasn’t the right space for it, is what troubles me theologically about that flattening.

The claim that each person is made in the image of God, the imago Dei, is one of the oldest and strangest affirmations in the Christian tradition, strange because of what it implies: that there is a particularity about each person, this one with this history, that reflects something true about God. And if that’s right, then the pressure to sound like everyone else isn’t just a communications problem or a content strategy problem. It isn’t even a cultural problem, not primarily. It’s a kind of conformity that has some spiritual weight to it.

I want to be careful here, because this argument can go wrong, tipping into the kind of therapeutic individualism that baptises every personal preference as sacred and every eccentricity as God-given, the kind that treats resistance to accountability as spiritual authenticity. That’s not what I mean.

What I mean is more specific, and it’s about motivation. When the flattening comes from anxiety, from the fear of being less visible and less relevant, out of step with whatever the algorithm is currently rewarding, what gets surrendered is presence. The way you’ve come to understand and articulate what you actually think, formed over years of experience, reading, doubt, conversation and failure, and the ease with which all of it can be swapped for something statistically averaged: at the very least, that’s worth sitting with.

Paul writes to the Romans about transformation, do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, and the word he uses for conformed is syschematizesthe, which carries the sense of being pressed into a mould, external pressure reshaping form. I doubt Paul had LinkedIn in mind, but the shape of the thing is recognisable enough.

I’ve been doing my own version of this work for a couple of years now, trying to document what I actually sound like well enough that I can be helped toward it rather than away from it. It’s been an ongoing project, undisciplined at times, with longer stretches where it sat on the back burner than I’d like to admit, and genuinely revealing in the moments I’ve paid it real attention. I kept finding the same things: the same instincts and rhythms, the same places where the thinking sharpens and the places where it drifts, and that recognition feels less like content strategy and more like stewardship.

The prophets in the Hebrew Bible were not interchangeable, Amos sounding nothing like Isaiah, Jeremiah sounding nothing like either of them, each arriving with a voice shaped by the life he’d actually lived. That wasn’t incidental to what they had to say. It was load-bearing, part of the message itself. Amos was a shepherd and a dresser of sycamore trees, which he says in chapter seven of nine, almost defensively, in the middle of being told by a priest to go home and stop prophesying there. The roughness of that background is in every line. The message couldn’t be averaged. It had to come from somewhere specific.

That’s a long way from LinkedIn posts, I know. I’m not arguing that everyone needs to do the voice-mapping work I’ve been doing, or that AI tools are theologically suspect. I’m not arguing that authenticity is always more faithful than polish. Those would be tidy conclusions, and this doesn’t quite earn them.

What I’m saying is simpler, and it’s this: if you’re a person made in the image of a particular God, with a history and a set of questions and a way of arriving at things that belong to you and no one else, then the habit of reaching for the averaged version of yourself, because it’s faster or safer or less exposed, is worth naming. Not condemning, just naming, as a thing that’s happening, and a choice that’s being made.

The birdsong is still there

I don’t know what to tell you to do about it. Mine turns up most clearly when I’m writing about something I can’t be bothered to sound clever about, and it goes quiet in the weeks I’m most worried about being noticed.

Which makes the work mostly attention. Attention is what the feed is built to spend for you.
