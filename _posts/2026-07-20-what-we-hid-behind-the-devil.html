Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance to me, for you are setting your mind not on divine things but on human things. (Ironic?)

There is a line in The Usual Suspects lodged in my head, “the greatest trick the devil ever pulled was convincing the world he didn’t exist”, and I keep circling back to the fact that the film did not invent the thought, Quote Investigator traces it through Baudelaire with earlier Christian versions before him, so the line has already done the thing ideas do, passed around and sharpened and simplified and misremembered, which feels like part of the point, because I have been wondering if it works better the other way round, what if we convinced ourselves he did instead.

I am not trying to say evil is imaginary, I do not believe that for a second: abuse, violence, and cruelty are real and the damage institutions learn to protect is real too, and if evil is real I do not want a version of faith that gives me somewhere too convenient to put it.

“the greatest trick the devil ever pulled was convincing the world he didn’t exist”

Compression

I am not a Hebrew scholar, I am not going to spend years learning Greek and arguing with footnotes in German, but there are people who have done that work and some of them are now sharing it in books and podcasts and YouTube videos, which is one of the stranger gifts of living when we do: I can listen to people who have spent years pulling apart texts I inherited as though they were obvious, so I receive something that has already been decompressed by someone else. A scholar pulls apart a garment I had only ever seen as one piece and shows the threads: this bit is Job, Genesis, Isaiah, Enoch, Revelation, this bit is later Christian imagination, this bit is fear, and this bit is folklore, and once you have seen the threads you cannot quite go back to seeing only the garment.

That is how learning works most of the time, we take what we hear and reduce it and pass on the shape of it, and the issue is that we forget. Dan McClellan talks about lectio difficilior, the more difficult reading is often earlier, and the instinct behind it makes sense: when people copy texts they smooth the awkwardness and harmonise the tension and make the thing easier to hold, which feels true of tradition as well as memory. Frederic Bartlett showed how people reshape stories as they retell them, and fuzzy-trace theory gets at something similar, we remember gist more readily than exact detail, which is useful but after enough retellings the gist starts to feel like the original, and the simplified version feels biblical. It is like saving the same image as a JPEG again and again: the file gets smaller, the edges blur, but the picture is still recognisable, which is the danger because it is close enough that you stop noticing the artefacts. That is how the Devil looks to me now: an image saved and resaved until the artefacts became part of what people thought the picture was.

Heuristics

Rebecca Reynolds described Christianity as a heuristic, a mental shortcut, fast and experiential, trading perfect precision for something that works well enough in the moment, the kind of thing you use without noticing, choosing a restaurant by how many cars are in the car park. Her point was that faith has always worked something like this. Scrutiny is not how people actually arrive at trust: you try something and see whether it works and check the fruit, and the Bible itself seems comfortable with this, spending more energy saying walk in this, see how it works than building bulletproof proofs.

Jacques Haers made a related argument from the other direction, that hermeneutics at its most dynamic is already heuristic, meaning emerges from the mutual interaction of lived experience and received tradition, each deepening the other. I have been thinking about what this means for why the compressed Devil has been so durable, because if faith works heuristically then compression is how human cognition operates.

Marketing psychology is built on this: advertisers know you will use heuristics, the availability heuristic where a familiar brand feels important, the affect heuristic where a warm feeling means a good product, social proof where everyone believing something makes it reasonable, and the same shortcuts make compressed theology feel true. You have heard about the Devil since childhood, the image is familiar and emotionally satisfying, it gives you somewhere to put fear and confusion, everybody around you believes something similar, the shortcut says this must be right. A heuristic approach is what opened this for me, I started asking whether certain beliefs actually worked, whether they made me more alive or less alive, and some of them could not survive that question: the Devil as inherited was one, the shortcut was leading in the wrong direction. The method was not the problem. The compressed image was functioning as a shortcut for where to put evil, and like any shortcut left unexamined it could be exploited.

Threads

The Hebrew Bible has satan language but not the Devil most of us inherited. In Job, ha-satan is the accuser, a role in the heavenly court, not a cosmic villain whispering behind every bad thought, and Job does not become easier without the later Devil, it becomes harder, the accuser is not operating outside God’s world, Job’s friends reach for the moral tidiness religious people still reach for and Job refuses, he will not make his suffering fit their doctrine, and the book vindicates him for that refusal. Genesis is awkward in a different way, the serpent is not called Satan and the story never mentions a fallen angel entering the garden, it is only a serpent, and it is stranger than the Sunday school version: the serpent says their eyes will be opened and they are, the serpent says they will become like divine beings and God says the human has become like one of us, which makes the simple version look suspiciously smooth compared to what is actually there.

Lucifer is another thread. Isaiah 14 is a taunt against the king of Babylon, Ezekiel 28 aimed at the king of Tyre, human rulers with political arrogance in their own historical clothes, and later those texts become the Devil’s biography, and I get why the imagery is powerful and wants to travel, but something changes when a taunt against a king becomes the backstory of Satan: the earthly critique gets lifted into the heavens, the king disappears behind the myth. Then the other material gathers: Enoch’s Watchers, fallen angels, forbidden knowledge, demons and Beelzebub and Azazel, the dragon and the beast and the lake of fire, medieval art and sermons and films and nightmares, none of it arrives all at once, it accumulates.

Somewhere To Put It

The other part is psychology: human beings like external causes, if something is wrong it is easier to locate the source out there, in another person or a spiritual attack or a system, and sometimes it really is the system, but sometimes the language of outside causes lets me avoid what I am entangled in without wanting to name it. I know this instinct, it is easier to say I am under pressure than to admit I am afraid, easier to locate the problem outside than to sit with the possibility that I am part of it, and scale that up and it becomes more dangerous: a church can call abuse a spiritual attack and never ask why its structures protected the abuser, a movement can call queer people demonic and never ask why their flourishing feels threatening, a nation can call itself Christian while treating migrants and the poor as contamination. The Devil becomes useful there, he gives us somewhere to put what we do not want to confess, and that does not require a deliberate doctrine of scapegoating, we rarely need to be that organised: people compress and externalise and inherit stories and find them useful, and then the usefulness becomes tradition, and somewhere in that process the distinction between what serves us and what is true blurs.

Empire, Hell, and Fear

Revelation is where this becomes most uncomfortable because I grew up around end-times Christianity, enough that Revelation sat in my imagination as a coded map of future terrors involving beasts and marks and numbers and someone explaining why the news had finally caught up with prophecy. Then scholars put it back in its own world: Rome, empire, worship, communities trying to stay faithful under pressure, and Brian Blount’s reading that Revelation is apocalyptic resistance literature, using nightmare to say Caesar is not Lord, has been sitting with me, because if I turn Revelation into a future-devil timetable I may miss the beast in front of me, and beastliness happens whenever domination asks for worship and violence calls itself peace, whenever power wraps itself in sacred language and expects the church to nod along, which is more frightening than a monster because it is closer.

Hell has gone through its own compression, Sheol and Gehenna and Hades and Greek philosophy and medieval imagination and Dante and sermons and youth-group fear all folded together with Satan placed in the middle as though he has always been there, but Sheol is not Dante’s inferno, the Hebrew Bible’s grave is not the later Christian hell, Revelation’s lake of fire is not the whole doctrine of eternal conscious torment. Not about making everything comfortable, it is about noticing how much has been squeezed into one image and what that image can do to a person: fear becomes holy, punishment becomes infinite, God stays good while the architecture of terror remains intact, and the Devil absorbs some of the horror but the system remains, which is the bit I cannot unsee.

Uncompressing

Deconstruction is often described as pulling faith apart, and sometimes that is fair, there are things I cannot rebuild and I do not want to, but much of this has felt like opening a compressed file, slowly, sometimes with the wrong software and a lot of error messages, the single image becomes threads again, and that can feel like loss but also like relief because evil has not disappeared, it becomes harder to ignore. I cannot simply blame the Devil for abuse, I have to ask who had power and who was not believed and which theology made silence feel holy, I cannot read Revelation as a monster map and ignore empire, I cannot use hell to make fear sound like faith, and I do not think the way forward is disbelief in evil, that would be far too easy. I think the way forward is refusing to outsource it.
