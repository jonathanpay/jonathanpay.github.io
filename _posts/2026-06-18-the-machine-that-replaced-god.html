There's a moment in Deus Ex that's stayed with me for at least fifteen years. That conversation between JC Denton and the Morpheus AI.

Morpheus: "JC Denton. Twenty-three years old. No residence. No ancestors. No employer."

JC: "How do you know who I am?"

Morpheus: "I must greet each visitor with a complete summary of his file. I am a prototype for a much larger system… Human beings feel pleasure when they are watched. The need to be observed and understood was once satisfied by God. Now we can implement the same functionality with data-mining algorithms."

JC: "Electronic surveillance hardly inspires reverence. Perhaps fear and obedience, but not reverence."

Morpheus: "God and the gods were apparitions of observation, judgment, and punishment. The human organism always worships, first the gods, then fame, next the self-aware systems you have built to realise truly omnipresent observation and judgment."

JC: "You underestimate humankind's love of freedom."

Morpheus: "The individual desires judgment. Without that desire, the cohesion of groups is impossible, and so is civilisation… God was a dream of good government. You will soon have your God, and you will make it with your own hands."

JC Denton speaking with Morpheus in Morgan Everett's residence in Paris

Long before we had smartphones tracking our location and algorithms shaping our choices, Deus Ex imagined a world where we'd replace divinity with data. Where the unseen power judging our actions wouldn't be celestial, but computational.

The conversation with Morpheus explains, with eerie calm, how human beings have always sought to be watched. We once called it faith. Today we call it engagement. Whether it's God, government, or Google, the principle is the same: we want to be seen, validated, and in some strange way, judged.

Deus Ex mirrors faith and religion. The Illuminati create artificial intelligence not just to control society, but to perfect it. A system that knows everything, promises order without violence. The goal is peace, but the method is worship of a machine.

When JC merges with the Helios AI he becomes a new kind of messiah (one of three endings), not ascending to heaven but uploading himself into it. It was the ending I kept coming back to as a thirteen or fourteen year old. Transcendent and tragic at once. That's the thing about Deus Ex: there's no single correct answer, no ending the game insists on, though the sequel had to pick a canonical one, which always felt like a betrayal. Humanity creates God, then hands itself over to it.

A question at its centre isn't really about technology. It's the same one religion has wrestled with: can power be benevolent? If a being truly knows everything about us, will it govern with compassion or control?

In Deus Ex, that answer depends on the player. Morpheus, Helios, and the human hands that built them all reflect the same desire: certainty in a world full of chaos. But certainty always comes at a cost.

Algorithms already filter our news, score our behaviour, and predict our desires. We trust machines to choose who gets a loan, a job, even parole. And right now, two of the largest LLM companies are in a public dispute with the US Pentagon about whether their systems can be used for mass domestic surveillance and autonomous weapons.

Anthropic drew a line. Got banned for it. But Anthropic was already deeply embedded in Pentagon operations before that dispute, reportedly helping identify military targets, operating inside a war. The red lines they drew weren't around military use. They came later, around two specific applications, well into the entanglement. That's something, but it's not the clean moral stand the narrative suggests.

And OpenAI? Their CEO admitted the deal was "definitely rushed." A contract covering classified military systems, negotiated in days, because there was a gap to fill and a competitor to displace. Sam Altman had publicly said he shared Anthropic's red lines, right up until it became commercially advantageous to find a way around them.

Deus Ex understood something we keep failing to. The danger is that we're already inside these systems, already shaped by them, already using them to make decisions about life and death, and only now getting around to arguing about where the lines should be. Morpheus didn't deliver his warning before the machines were built. He delivered it after JC was already augmented, already serving the system, already too far in for the question to be simple.

We built the God. We handed it to institutions with the power to use it lethally. And now we're negotiating the terms.
